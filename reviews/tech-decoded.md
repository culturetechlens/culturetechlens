# 1. Keyboards Decoded

## Past → present → future: the typewriter is still in the room

A typewriter key had to move far and hit hard, and you knew — by feel and by sound — whether the letter had landed. Early computer keyboards kept that bargain. IBM's Model M, introduced in 1985, used a buckling-spring switch under each key: press, the spring buckles, you get a clear bump and a click at the same moment the press registers. Typists trained on typewriters had come to expect that level of feedback, and many Model Ms are still working decades later.

Then cost won for a while. Cheaper rubber-dome and membrane boards took over offices in the 1990s — IBM stopped producing the Model M in 1996 amid that industry-wide switch — and typing got quieter, softer, and, for a lot of hands, mushier.

The present is a split screen. Most laptops use thin scissor switches. Most desks that care use mechanical switches again. And the future arriving now senses a key without the parts even touching: a magnet and a sensor (Hall effect), or a beam of light (optical). Same old question as the typewriter, new way of answering it: *did my press land, and how do I know?*

## The six kinds, in plain words

**Mechanical.** Every key has its own switch: a spring, a stem, and metal contacts that meet when you press. That is why one key can feel completely different from the same-looking key on another board — change the spring and stem, change the feel. Kinesis, which has made mechanical ergonomic boards since 1992, rates its professional switches for 50 million keystrokes, and points to feel, precision, and durability as the reasons people pay more for them. The trade-offs are height, cost, and — depending on the switch — noise.

**Membrane.** One rubber sheet runs under the whole board. Under each key is a small rubber dome; press it down and it pushes two conductive layers together to register the press. Membrane boards are budget-friendly, quiet, and more resistant to dust and spills. The cost of that simplicity is feel and lifespan: they can feel mushy and less responsive, and the sheet wears out sooner than individual switches.

**Scissor-switch.** The laptop keyboard. Two little crossed arms — the scissors — steady the key over a rubber dome, so the key goes straight down in a very short trip. That short travel is why laptops can be thin, and why the keys feel snappy but shallow. Kinesis notes the other side of that coin: short travel can mean your fingertips take more impact, tactility is limited, and lifespan is shorter than mechanical. If you like your laptop keyboard, you like scissor switches. If your fingers ache after a long day on one, that short trip is a prime suspect.

**Low-profile mechanical.** A real mechanical switch, made shorter. Cherry's low-profile MX and its earlier half-sized ML switches are the textbook examples: same idea — individual switch, spring, contact — in a thinner body with a shorter trip down. You get mechanical choice and feel in a board that sits lower on the desk and asks less wrist bend to reach. What you give up is some of that deep, full-travel feel, and the parts are less interchangeable with standard-height switches and keycaps.

**Hall effect.** No contact at all. Each key stem carries a small magnet; a sensor on the board watches the magnetic field get stronger as the key moves down, so the board knows *how far* the key has travelled, not just *whether* it crossed one fixed point. That buys three real things: you can set the actuation point yourself — shallow for fast game inputs, deeper to stop accidental presses while typing; a key can reset the instant you start to lift it (sold as “rapid trigger”); and there are no metal contacts to wear or bounce. The technology is old in hard places — Hall effect keyboards were built in the 1960s for aerospace and military use because they survived heat, cold, vibration, dust, and moisture — and new again in gaming boards. Vendor ratings of 100 million-plus presses are common for these switches; treat any single brand's number as that brand's rating, not a promise about every board.

**Optical.** Also no metal contact, but the messenger is light instead of a magnet. Press the key and the stem lets an infrared beam through; the beam reaching the sensor *is* the press. Razer, for its optical switches, lists 45 grams of actuation force, actuation points of 1.5 mm (clicky) and 1.2 mm (linear), and a 100 million-click durability rating, and says optical actuation avoids the bounce found in traditional mechanical switches because there is no contact to bounce. In lived terms: optical and Hall effect are cousins. Both remove the touching metal. Optical mostly gives you a fixed press, sensed instantly. Hall effect gives you a measured press, so it can do more tricks with depth.

## How a switch feels: four words that matter

Forget the color names for a second. Switch colors are brand shorthand, not a standard — though many makers copy Cherry's colors. Feel comes in four families:

- **Linear** — smooth all the way down, no bump. Cherry MX Red is the classic example: 45 grams, linear. Gamers who press the same key fast, over and over, often like that nothing gets in the way.
- **Tactile** — smooth until a small bump tells your finger “that landed,” then smooth again. Cherry MX Brown: 55 grams, tactile. That bump is a kindness to typists: you can learn to stop pressing once you feel it, instead of slamming every key to the bottom.
- **Clicky** — tactile bump *plus* a deliberate click sound. Cherry MX Blue: 60 grams, tactile with an audible click. Wonderful alone in a room. A relationship test in a shared one.
- **Silent** — usually a linear or tactile switch with rubber dampeners built in to swallow the sound of the key going down and coming back up. Cherry's Silent Red is a damped Red at 45 grams. Kailh's silent pink switch for one Kinesis board is even lighter, at 35 grams. Silent does not mean soundless — you will still hear a soft thock — it means the sharp clack is gone.

**Actuation force, translated.** This is just “how hard do I have to push,” measured in grams. About 35–45 grams is a light push: a resting finger almost does it, which is easy on tired hands but easy to trigger by accident. Around 55–60 grams is a firm push: fewer stray letters, more work for your fingers over a long day. There is no correct number. There is only the number your hands can do for four hours without complaining.

## Layouts: what you lose as the board shrinks

Percentages are rough shorthand for how much of a full-size board is left, not an exact measurement. Key counts below are the common US versions.

- **Full-size (100%, about 104 keys).** Everything: letters, function row, navigation cluster, and the number pad on the right. Best if you enter numbers for a living. Worst for your mouse shoulder — that number pad pushes your mouse hand far out to the side.
- **TKL — tenkeyless (about 87 keys).** Full-size minus the number pad. Function row, arrows, and navigation keys all stay where your memory expects them. This is the safest shrink: more desk, mouse closer to your body, almost nothing to relearn.
- **75% (about 80–84 keys).** A TKL squeezed tight: function row and arrows kept, gaps between groups mostly gone, some navigation keys moved or shared. Compact and complete, but the tight packing takes a few days to learn.
- **65% (about 66–68 keys).** Arrows stay — that is the whole point of 65% over 60% — but the function row goes onto a second layer: hold Fn and press a number-row key to get an F-key.
- **60% (about 61 keys).** Letters, numbers, and modifiers, and that is nearly it. Arrows and F-keys live on Fn layers. Smallest, most portable, most homework. Be honest about whether you will actually learn the layers.
- **Split / ergonomic.** The board comes in two halves you can place at shoulder width, angle, and sometimes tent upward, so your wrists stay straight instead of bending outward to meet a straight board. Some split boards also reshape the keys into wells. The gain is posture; the price is an adjustment period while your hands relearn where things are. If typing hurts, this family — not a fancier switch — is the first place to look.

## Three specs, translated into lived terms

**Hot-swap.** On a hot-swappable board you can pull a switch out with a small tool and push a different one in — no soldering iron, no wrecking the board. In lived terms: one key feels wrong, or dies, or is too loud for the room you moved into, and you change that one switch in a minute instead of buying a whole new keyboard. Not every mechanical board is hot-swap; some are soldered. Check before you buy if changing switches is part of your plan.

**Key rollover.** How many keys the board reports correctly when several are down at once. “6-key rollover” covers most typing and a lot of gaming. “N-key rollover” (NKRO) means every key can register at once. You feel rollover when a combination silently fails — a game move that never happened, a shortcut that half-fired. Fast typists who overlap presses, and gamers holding run + strafe + jump + ability, are the people who actually need NKRO.

**Polling rate.** How often the board reports to the computer, in times per second (Hz). 1,000 Hz means a report every millisecond. 8,000 Hz boards exist — Razer pairs that rate with its optical switches, and Hall effect gaming boards advertise it too. Honest proportion: going from a basic board to 1,000 Hz removes a delay most people can just barely sense in fast games. Going from 1,000 to 8,000 shrinks a delay that is already one millisecond. Competitive players chase it; typists will never feel it. Do not pay a typist's money for a sprinter's spec.

## Decide before you buy

- **How loud can it be where you type?** Shared office, sleeping baby, late-night apartment — start with silent switches or a membrane/scissor board, not clicky. Alone with a door that closes? Click if it makes you happy.
- **Do your wrists, hands, or shoulders hurt?** Pain points to shape before switch: a split or angled board, a smaller layout that brings the mouse in close, and a light actuation force. A new switch in a board that bends your wrists will not fix your wrists.
- **Do you have low vision?** Look first at the letters, not the specs: large, high-contrast legends you can read at a glance, and backlighting that lights the legends themselves. Mechanical and scissor boards can both be backlight-compatible; many membrane boards are not. Try to see the exact board lit, in a dim room, before you commit.
- **Do you type numbers all day?** Keep the number pad (full-size) or buy a compact board plus a separate pad you can put on either side.
- **Do you want to tinker, or never think about it again?** Tinkerers want hot-swap and a 65% or 75% board. Set-and-forget buyers want a TKL that matches the layout already in their hands.

# 2. Computers Decoded

## Past → present → future: from one room to every pocket — and back to the room

At the start of the 1970s, a serious computer was a room-sized mainframe, built one at a time, run by specialists; most people never touched one directly — they handed in punch cards and collected printouts later, or shared the machine through time-sharing terminals. The personal computer revolution was the revolt against that: one person, one machine, on your own desk, answering to you.

The present looks like that revolt won completely. A mini PC smaller than a lunchbox does office work that once needed the room.

But look at the future thread honestly: a lot of computing has walked *back* to the shared room. Your files, photos, and even AI assistants often live on somebody else's big computers and arrive over the internet — time-sharing with better manners. A Chromebook is built on exactly that bet. So the real question in 2026 is not “laptop or desktop?” It is: **how much of your computer do you want in your house, and how much are you renting from the cloud?**

## The shapes: what each form factor really trades

- **Tower desktop.** The big open box. Its one superpower is change: standard parts inside, room to swap the graphics card, add drives, add memory, replace a power supply. If you want a machine you can grow instead of replace, this is it. You pay in floor/desk space, noise, and the fact that you also need a monitor, keyboard, and mouse.
- **Mini PC.** The same desktop idea shrunk to a small box that can hide behind a monitor. Fine for office work, browsing, streaming, and light creation, quiet, and easy on power. The shrink has a cost, and it is upgradeability: many mini PCs let you change the memory and the SSD and nothing else — processor and graphics are soldered in for life — and a small case has less room to get rid of heat during long, heavy jobs.
- **All-in-one.** The computer built into the back of its own screen. Cleanest desk in the world: one plug, no box. The trade is the tower's trade in reverse — it mostly uses laptop-style parts, upgrades are usually limited to memory and storage at best, and if the screen dies, your computer is down with it.
- **Laptop.** Screen, keyboard, battery, and computer in one folding package. The default computer for most people, and the right one if the machine has to move between rooms, jobs, schools, or cities. You pay for the shrinking: less power for the money than a tower, parts increasingly soldered in, and a screen and keyboard you cannot easily replace if you stop liking them.
- **2-in-1.** A laptop whose screen folds all the way back, or detaches, so it can pretend to be a tablet. Good for marking up documents, drawing, presenting standing up, and watching in a tent fold on a tray table. As a pure laptop it usually costs more than the same power in a regular shape, and as a pure tablet it is heavier than one.
- **Chromebook.** A laptop-shaped machine running Google's ChromeOS, built around the Chrome browser and cloud storage. Best Buy's school guide puts the lived difference plainly: web apps and Google services run naturally, files live in Google Drive, and the machine boots fast; but full desktop programs — the complete versions of tools like Adobe's creative apps or specialized school/work software — may not run at all, web versions may have fewer features, local storage is modest (often 64–128 GB in that guide), and offline work needs setting up ahead of time. A Chromebook is not a cheap laptop. It is a different answer: the cloud is the computer, this is the door.
- **Workstation.** A desktop or laptop built and certified for professional work — design, engineering, video, science, large data — with an emphasis on sustained power, lots of memory, professional graphics, and parts chosen to run all day without quitting. If your income stops when the machine stutters, this category exists for you. If it doesn't, you are likely paying for stamina you will never use.
- **Gaming PC.** A tower (or laptop) built around one priority: a strong graphics card, with the cooling and power supply to feed it. Everything else — lights, cases, branding — is decoration on that one decision. A gaming PC is also, not by accident, a very capable machine for video editing, 3D work, and running AI tools locally, because all of those jobs lean on the same graphics muscle.

## The parts, in lived-use terms

**CPU — the manager.** The central processing unit runs the show: it takes instructions, does general-purpose work, and tells every other part what to do next. Britannica's history calls the microprocessor “the brain of the personal desktop computer — the central processing unit, or CPU.” Think manager, not crew: a good manager keeps ten different jobs moving without dropping one. For everyday use, manager quality shows up as *responsiveness* — windows open now, not in a second — more than as any number on the box.

**GPU — the visual crew.** The graphics processing unit draws the picture: games, video, 3D, effects. HDMI.org's explainer is blunt about the division of labor — the GPU “does most of the heavy lifting, particularly at higher resolutions and refresh rates,” taking the scene the CPU describes and rendering it into the frames you see. A CPU has a few strong workers doing jobs one after another; a GPU has a huge crew doing the same simple job all at once. That is why the GPU decides how a game looks and runs, why video and 3D work lean on it, and — remember this for the NPU section — why serious local AI tools today mostly run on GPUs.

Two kinds: *integrated* graphics live inside the CPU and share the machine's memory — fine for office work, streaming, and light games. A *dedicated* (discrete) graphics card is its own board with its own memory — the expensive route, and the only route to high-end gaming or heavy visual work on a tower.

**RAM — desk space.** Random access memory is the workspace for everything happening *right now*. HP's own analogy is the one worth keeping: RAM is the desk, storage is the filing cabinet. A bigger desk means more projects open at once without a trip to the cabinet for every page. Too little RAM and the machine keeps shuffling work back to the much slower storage, which is when a computer feels choked and stuttery with many tabs and apps open. RAM is cleared when the power goes off — nothing lives there. HP lists common modern amounts as 8–32 GB or more; in plain buying terms, 8 GB is a small desk, 16 GB is the comfortable desk most people should aim for, and 32 GB-plus is for big creative, technical, or gaming loads.

**SSD — the filing cabinet.** The solid-state drive is long-term storage: your system, programs, and files, kept when the machine is off, in an internal drive with no moving parts to spin up or wear like an old hard disk. HP puts typical storage at 256 GB to multiple terabytes, far larger than RAM and far slower to reach into — which is exactly the desk-and-cabinet relationship. A full cabinet is its own misery: when an SSD is nearly full, everything that needs to file something new has nowhere to put it. Buy more cabinet than you think you need if you keep photos, video, or a game library.

**Ports and Thunderbolt — the doors, and one very good door.** A port's *shape* and a port's *speed* are different facts, and the industry hides the second behind the first. USB-C is just the small oval plug shape. Thunderbolt is a faster standard that uses that same plug: Thunderbolt 4 is guaranteed at 40 Gbps; Thunderbolt 5 doubles that to 80 Gbps, can push up to 120 Gbps in one direction for displays, and brings up to 240 watts of power delivery. In lived terms, one good Thunderbolt cable can carry power *in*, picture *out* to one or two monitors, and files to a fast external drive, through a single dock — your whole desk plugged in with one hand. But a plain USB-C port on the same-looking machine may do far less. The lightning-bolt mark is the tell; when in doubt, read the spec line for that exact port, not the shape of the hole. For most single- or dual-monitor desks, Thunderbolt 4 is already enough; Thunderbolt 5 earns its keep moving enormous files or driving several high-resolution, high-refresh screens.

## “AI PC” and the NPU — graded, not sold

**VERIFIED FACT.** An NPU (neural processing unit) is a specialized chip for AI-style math — the pattern-matching calculations behind features like background blur, voice cleanup, live captions, and image recognition. Microsoft defines its Copilot+ PC class by it: an NPU that can perform more than 40 trillion operations per second (TOPS), powering named Windows experiences including Recall, Cocreator, Windows Studio Effects, Live Captions, and automatic super resolution. TOPS is a speed rating for that kind of math, like a top speed for one specific road.

**STRONG EVIDENCE — what it actually accelerates today.** A short, real list: camera and microphone effects during calls (blur, eye-contact correction, voice focus), live captions and translation, and the specific built-in Windows features above, plus whichever individual apps a maker has explicitly written to use the NPU. The genuine advantage is efficiency, not raw power: an NPU can keep those always-on jobs running at low power instead of waking the hungrier GPU, which is a battery-life story on a laptop.

**CONTESTED — and the contest is the story.** Does that make an “AI PC” worth choosing *for* the NPU today? PCWorld's two-year verdict was harsh: the big NPU push “failed spectacularly” at delivering the promised powerful local AI, the most popular local AI tools (it names LM Studio, Ollama, and Llama.cpp) run on GPUs and, at the time of that review, could not use NPUs at all, and the built-in Copilot+ features struck the reviewer as small perks next to the marketing. Other reviewers land softer — the features above are real and pleasant, and the software net is widening — but nobody honest disputes the narrowness of today's list, the fragmentation across Intel, AMD, and Qualcomm NPUs, or that a strong GPU remains the faster tool for heavy local AI.

**CTL grade: RESEARCH, don't pay a premium on a promise.** “AI PC” is a **marketing term** for a class of machines, not a guarantee that the AI you care about runs on the NPU. If a laptop is right for you on screen, keyboard, battery, and price, its NPU is a free option on the future. If a seller is charging extra *for* the AI label, ask one question — *name the app I use every week that runs on this NPU* — and grade the silence. And note the label trap: not every machine sold as an “AI PC” meets the 40+ TOPS Copilot+ bar; some carry much weaker NPUs that cannot run those Copilot+ experiences at all.

## Upgradeability and the real 5-year cost

The sticker is year one. The machine is five years. Think in totals:

- **What can you change later?** On a tower: nearly everything, one part at a time. On many mini PCs and all-in-ones: memory and SSD, sometimes, and nothing else. On many modern laptops and Chromebooks: possibly nothing — memory and storage soldered at the factory. A part you cannot upgrade is a decision you cannot undo, so on a sealed machine you must buy the RAM and SSD you will need in year four, today.
- **What will you add anyway?** A laptop that lives at a desk usually grows a monitor, a keyboard, a dock, and a backup drive. A tower already has the desk parts counted separately. A Chromebook that runs out of local room grows a cloud-storage subscription. Count the entourage, not just the machine.
- **What ends its life?** Machines rarely die all at once; they get abandoned when one fixed part — a full SSD you can't swap, a battery that no longer holds a charge, an operating system whose updates stop (Chromebooks carry an update end date worth checking on the exact model) — makes the whole thing feel finished. Upgradeable machines let you fix the one tired part. Sealed machines ask you to replace the whole computer.
- **Electricity and repairs count, too — direction, not decimals.** A mini PC sipping power all day costs less to run than a big tower under load, and one repairable part costs less than one whole replacement. You don't need a spreadsheet to use this: the cheapest five years usually belong to the machine you *don't* have to replace in year three.

## Decide before you buy

- **Does the machine have to move?** Daily, weekly, or never? Never is a tower or mini PC's home turf; daily means a laptop and accepting sealed parts.
- **What is the one program that must run?** Name it. If it is a full desktop program, a Chromebook is out no matter how nice the screen is. If it is a game, the GPU decides, not the CPU. If it lives in a browser, almost everything on this page can run it.
- **Will you want more in three years — more storage, more screens, a better graphics card?** If yes, buy doors: a tower, or a machine with Thunderbolt and upgradeable memory/storage.
- **Are you being sold an “AI PC”?** Apply the test above: which of *your* weekly apps runs on the NPU, today, in writing?
- **Do you need to see bigger, not faster?** Many “my computer is slow” complaints are really “my screen is small and dim.” A good separate monitor can outlive two laptops.

# 3. TVs Decoded

## Past → present → future: the deep box, the flat picture, the wall itself

The old TV was furniture with a tube in it. A cathode-ray tube (CRT) fires electron beams at a phosphor-coated screen inside a glass envelope that is, by design, heavy, fragile, and long from front to back — the tube *is* most of a CRT TV's weight, and about 40–45 inches was as big as one could practically get. Since the late 2000s, flat panels — LCD, LED-backlit LCD, plasma, OLED — have replaced it: lighter, thinner, wall-mountable, far larger.

The present is a label war on top of two real families: screens lit from behind, and screens where each pixel lights itself. The future has a name — Micro LED — and for most households it is still a future: real, demonstrated, and priced like a car.

## The label tree, made honest

Ask one question of every TV name: **where does the light come from?**

**LED-LCD — a backlight behind a shutter.** “LED TV” is the base case, and the name is already a small trick: the picture is made by an LCD panel — a layer that opens and closes to let light through, pixel by pixel — and the LEDs are only the lamp behind it. Samsung's own guide says it straight: LED simply refers to the backlight. Black on an LCD is a shutter blocking a lamp that is still on, which is why LCD blacks often look dark grey in a dark room.

**QLED — a color layer on that same LCD. Marketing-layer label.** QLED adds a layer of quantum dots — nanocrystals that emit different colors depending on their particle size — between backlight and panel, for greater brightness and more accurate color than an average LCD. A real improvement in color; not a new kind of screen. A QLED is an LED-LCD with a better color coat, named to sit next to OLED on the shelf and sound like its peer. Judge it as a good LCD, not a self-lit screen. Other brands run the same play under their own quantum-dot names — the letters change, the backlight doesn't.

**Mini-LED — the same LCD, with a far better lamp.** Mini-LEDs are much smaller backlight LEDs — Samsung describes its Quantum Mini LEDs as 1/40th the size of traditional LEDs — so a TV fits far more of them, controlled in far more, smaller groups. That is *local dimming* in practice: the lamp dims behind dark parts of a scene and stays bright behind bright parts, zone by zone. More, smaller zones means deeper blacks and less glowing halo (“blooming”) around a bright object on a dark background. It never reaches per-pixel control — a zone is a neighborhood, not a house — but a good Mini-LED set comes closest to self-lit contrast of any LCD, and usually gets brighter than OLED.

**OLED — every pixel makes its own light.** Organic light-emitting diode: each pixel produces its own light and color, no backlight at all, so a pixel that should be black simply turns off — “real” black, and contrast no backlight TV can copy. The costs are real too: OLEDs generally don't reach the peak brightness of the best backlit sets (bright rooms, below), and the organic material is why burn-in exists as a topic (graded, below).

**QD-OLED — OLED, with quantum dots doing the coloring.** Samsung Display's QD-OLED starts with blue OLED light at every pixel and uses a quantum-dot layer to convert part of that blue into red and green, with no color-filter layer and no white subpixel. The other main route (LG's, often called WOLED) starts with white OLED light passed through red, green, and blue color filters. Both are self-lit; both turn pixels fully off. QD-OLED's calling card is saturated color that stays strong at high brightness; WOLED's is a longer production history and wider range of sizes. A family argument inside OLED, not a different species.

**Micro LED — the endgame, not yet the purchase.** Millions of microscopic, inorganic, self-emitting red, green, and blue LED chips: each pixel its own light like OLED, but inorganic — which is why Samsung's modular Micro LED line, The Wall, is built from tiles that combine into very large, custom-sized displays, pitched on brightness, perfect blacks, and long life with no organic material to wear. All true as technology. As shopping, today it belongs to luxury and commercial installations. Don't let a salesperson borrow its halo for a Mini-LED set — similar names, different machines.

**“AI upscaling” — marketing term.** Every modern processor claims some version of it (Samsung advertises “4K AI Upscaling”). Upscaling itself is necessary: most of what you watch arrives below 4K and must be stretched to fit. A better processor guesses the missing detail more convincingly. No processor can recover detail that was never in the signal. Test any “AI picture” claim on the low-quality source you actually watch — cable, old shows, a bad-night stream — not the store demo loop.

## HDR: the formats are instructions, not picture quality

HDR (high dynamic range) means a wider gap between darkest and brightest parts of the picture, with more color steps inside it. Consumer Reports, which measures HDR in its own lab tests, makes the buyer's point: HDR can make a picture much more lifelike, but not all sets with an HDR badge do it well — panel brightness and dimming do the heavy lifting; the format is the instruction sheet.

- **HDR10** — the baseline every HDR TV supports: *one* set of brightness instructions for the whole program (static metadata).
- **Dolby Vision** — adjusts scene by scene, even frame by frame (dynamic metadata), so dark and bright scenes each get their own instructions. Dolby's licensed format: a TV either paid for it or doesn't have it.
- **HDR10+** — the rival dynamic format, doing the same scene-by-scene job; the one Samsung backs, which is why Samsung TVs carry HDR10+ instead of Dolby Vision.
- **HLG** — the broadcast flavor, for live TV, backward-compatible with older sets.

Lived rule: you don't buy a format, you buy a TV that has or lacks one. A set *without* Dolby Vision plays those streamed titles in HDR10 — good, not crippled — but won't use the scene-by-scene instructions they carry. Check the format list against the services you pay for, then spend your real attention on brightness and local dimming.

## Motion: native refresh is the real number

**Native refresh rate** is how many times per second the panel physically draws a new picture: 60 Hz or 120 Hz on virtually every consumer TV. It is a hardware fact.

**“Motion Rate” — marketing term.** So are its cousins (TruMotion, MotionFlow XR, “effective refresh rate”). These bundle the native rate with software motion interpolation — the TV inventing in-between frames — and are typically double the real rate: “Motion Rate 120” typically means a native 60 Hz panel; “Motion Rate 240,” a native 120 Hz panel. The invented frames have a look: motion so smooth it turns film into the *soap opera effect*, plus artifacts around fast movement and extra delay gamers feel as input lag. You can turn the smoothing down or off on essentially every set.

Buying rule: find “native” or “panel” refresh in the detailed specs and ignore the big number on the box. Ordinary TV lives happily at 60 Hz native; sports lovers and gamers playing at 120 frames per second want a true 120 Hz panel — which brings us to the ports.

## HDMI 2.1, VRR, and ALLM: the gaming contract

A port version decides what can even arrive. HDMI 2.1 raises bandwidth to up to 48 Gbps, which makes 4K at up to 120 Hz possible, along with 8K at 60 Hz. Two features ride on it, both for games:

- **VRR (Variable Refresh Rate)** lets the TV match its refresh to the game as the frame rate rises and falls — frames delivered as soon as they are ready, per HDMI.org, reducing or eliminating lag, judder, and frame tearing. Smooth, without the TV inventing frames.
- **ALLM (Auto Low Latency Mode)** tells the TV “a game is playing,” so it switches itself into its fastest, lowest-delay mode.

Check the count, not just the badge: many TVs offer HDMI 2.1 on only some ports, and a console, a PC, and a soundbar over eARC fill those fast.

## Size vs. distance, bright room vs. dark room

**Size is a distance question.** Sit with your eyes about 1.2 to 1.6 times the screen's diagonal away — closer (about 40° of your field of view, the THX-style cinema end) for movies and games, farther (about 30°, the relaxed SMPTE-style end) for everyday TV. In numbers: 55-inch, about 5 ft 6 in to 7 ft 5 in; 65-inch, about 6 ft 6 in to 8 ft 10 in; 75-inch, about 7 ft 6 in to 10 ft 2 in. Past the far end, the 4K detail you paid for stops reaching your eyes. Measure where your head rests, not the front of the couch, and center the screen near seated eye level.

**Bright room vs. dark room is the other half of the panel choice.** In a bright room a TV needs raw brightness and good reflection handling to hold contrast — home turf for good Mini-LED / QLED sets, which Samsung itself frames as the brightness leaders, with OLED recommended by that same guide for darker rooms. In a dark room the fight reverses: a backlight's grey-ish blacks and blooming stand out, and OLED's true black takes the night. Match the screen to the light it will live in; if your room is bright *and* your viewing is mostly night movies, the specific model's anti-glare treatment matters as much as its panel family.

## Burn-in, in honest proportion — contested, graded

Burn-in is a permanent ghost of a static image — a news ticker, a game health bar, a logo — left by pixels worn unevenly. It applies to OLED because its light-makers are organic and wear with use; backlit LCDs do not burn in this way.

**What the strongest test says.** RTINGS ran more than 100 TVs at maximum brightness for years, showing a news channel with a constant ticker — roughly 10,000 hours and beyond. In that deliberate-abuse setting, burn-in appeared on OLEDs, and static content at high brightness was exactly the trigger; varied, dynamic content was not the problem. Two findings keep the proportion honest: most sets survived the first 10,000 hours (over three years at eight hours a day) without catastrophic failure, and LCDs failed in their own ways — problems were most common in LED-backlit TVs *without* local dimming, where individual LEDs burned out. The test that proved burn-in is real also proved “LCD lasts forever” is not.

**CTL grade: real risk, narrow door.** For mixed viewing — movies, shows, sports, varied games — burn-in is unlikely to be what ends your TV's life. The risk concentrates where the test concentrated it: the *same* static element, at high brightness, for hours daily — one news channel all day, signage use, or an OLED as an all-day PC monitor with fixed toolbars. If that is you, an LCD is the honest pick. Keep OLEDs' built-in countermeasures (pixel refreshing, logo dimming, shifting) on, vary what you watch, and don't leave a paused static screen cooking. We would not repeat a maker's “burn-in is solved” line as fact — the RTINGS result is the evidence against settling it that cleanly.

## The smart part: ads, tracking, and how to turn them down

A smart TV is a computer that sells advertising, with a screen attached. The mechanism has a name: **ACR (automatic content recognition)** — software that identifies what is on screen, including from connected devices, and reports viewing data used to build advertising profiles. This is a settled legal record, not a rumor: Vizio settled with the FTC and the New Jersey attorney general for $2.2 million over ACR deployed without adequate disclosure — on by default on new TVs, remotely installed on previously sold TVs, viewing and device-level information collected, viewing profiles sold for measurement and targeted ads — while the notice was, in the FTC's account, vague and fleeting, and the opt-out sat several menus deep.

You cannot make a smart TV private by wishing, but you can turn its volume down:

- Turn off the ACR switch for your brand — Samsung: Viewing Information Services; LG: Live Plus; Vizio: Viewing Data; Roku: Privacy → Smart TV Experience (“use info from TV inputs”).
- Limit or reset the advertising ID, and turn off interest-based ads where offered.
- Be choosy about sign-ins: every app account on the TV is another profile tied to the same screen.
- The strong move: keep the TV off your network (or on a guest network) and feed it from a separate streaming box you control. The box has its own tracking to tame — no free lunch — but you have cut two watchers to one.

## Accessibility: check it on the exact model

Accessibility is real, specific, and uneven across models and apps — verify it on the set you are buying, not from a brand promise. Samsung's support pages list a good checklist for any brand:

- **Voice Guide** — the TV speaks menu options, channel, and volume changes aloud.
- **Audio / video description** — a spoken track describing the scene, *when the program provides it*; the TV can only play a description track that exists.
- **Captions** — on broadcasts and some apps, with size, color, and background adjustable, and an option to show them in a separate area. Samsung's own warning: many streaming apps run their *own* caption settings, so TV-menu captions may not turn on inside the app — check both places.
- **Reading the menus** — high-contrast menus, enlarged/zoomed text, grayscale, color inversion.
- **Hearing it your way** — multi-output audio (speakers and a Bluetooth headphone/hearing device at once, at different volumes) and sign-language zoom.

A TV you cannot follow is the wrong TV at any size.

## Decide before you buy

- **How bright is the room where it will actually live — day and night?** Bright and uncontrolled points to a bright Mini-LED/QLED set with good anti-glare. Dark and controlled points to OLED.
- **What sits on screen for hours?** One news channel, static dashboards, or an all-day desktop raises the burn-in flag; mixed entertainment largely clears it.
- **Do you play current consoles or a gaming PC?** Then the checklist is native 120 Hz, HDMI 2.1 *on enough ports*, VRR, and ALLM — and ignore every “motion rate” number on the way there.
- **How far is your head from the wall?** Measure it, divide by 1.2 and by 1.6, and shop inside that size range instead of defaulting to the biggest box in the aisle.
- **Whose HDR do you watch?** Mostly Dolby Vision services on a set that lacks Dolby Vision means settling for the HDR10 version of your favorite shows, forever.
- **Who needs to follow it — eyes, ears, or both?** Test Voice Guide, description, captions (in the TV *and* in your main app), and headphone-plus-speakers output on the exact model.
- **How will the voices sound?** Thin TVs have thin speakers. If dialogue is hard to follow, budget the soundbar as part of this decision.
