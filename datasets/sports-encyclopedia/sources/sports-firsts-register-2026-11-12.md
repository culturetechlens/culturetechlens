# African-American Firsts in Sports — Register

The verified register behind the CTL Sports Firsts package. Built November 12, 2026 from William Maxey's intake list "African-American Firsts: Sports" (26 rows), adjudicated at the head and verified against authoritative sources under GOV-004: no factual claim ships without a source supporting the exact claim, and a "first" is asserted only in the exact form its source supports.

**How to read this register.** Rows are in chronological order by the verified year of the first. Each row carries: the exact-form claim CultureTechLens stands behind, the GOV-004 grade, the graph status (HELD in the Black Cultural Knowledge Graph with its ID, or STAGED in this packet under a PROPOSED ID), what was corrected from the intake wording, and the sources. Where a claim survives only with a qualifier, the qualifier is part of the claim — stripping it changes the grade.

**Grades ledger (26 rows):** Verified 23 · Supported 1 · Provisional 1 · Disputed 1 · Unresolved 0. **Graph ledger:** HELD 17 · STAGED 9.

## 1875 — Oliver Lewis
- **Exact first:** Oliver Lewis, an African American jockey, was the first jockey to win the Kentucky Derby, riding Aristides to victory in the inaugural running on May 17, 1875.
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** HELD — CTL-E-6891 (Person). Also in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** Stands as written.
- **Source:** Kentucky Commission on Human Rights, "Oliver Lewis" — "Oliver Lewis (1856-1924), the African American jockey who rode the colt Aristides, won the first Kentucky Derby on May 17, 1875." https://kchr.ky.gov/Hall-of-Fame/Pages/Oliver-Lewis.aspx
- **Source:** Encyclopedia.com, "Lewis, Oliver" — Lewis "won the inaugural Kentucky Derby on May 17, 1875"; he was one of 13 Black jockeys in the 15-rider field. https://www.encyclopedia.com/education/news-wires-white-papers-and-books/lewis-oliver

## 1884 — Moses Fleetwood Walker
- **Exact first:** Moses Fleetwood Walker was the first openly Black player in Major League Baseball, debuting May 1, 1884 as catcher for the Toledo Blue Stockings of the American Association.
- **Grade:** Verified — in this exact form only. The unqualified form ("first Black major leaguer") is Disputed (William Edward White, 1879 — see 04-red-team.md).
- **Graph status:** HELD — CTL-E-6852 (Person). Also in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** CORRECTED. Intake read "Professional baseball player" — wrong. Black professionals predate Walker: Bud Fowler played in Organized Baseball from 1878. The claim is carried in major-league form, with "openly" load-bearing.
- **Source:** Society for American Baseball Research (SABR), "Harris: The legacy of Moses Fleetwood Walker" — Walker played catcher for Toledo in 1884 and "is acknowledged by historians at the National Baseball Hall of Fame to actually be the first [African-American to play in the Major Leagues]." https://sabr.org/latest/harris-the-legacy-of-moses-fleetwood-walker/
- **Source:** Encyclopedia.com / Dictionary of American Biography, "Walker, Moses Fleetwood ('Fleet')" — "On 1 May 1884 Walker became the first African American to play baseball at the major league level." https://www.encyclopedia.com/humanities/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/walker-moses-fleetwood-fleet
- **Source:** SABR Games Project — "May 17, 1878: Bud Fowler becomes the first Black player in Organized Baseball." https://sabr.org/gamesproj/game/may-17-1878-bud-fowler-becomes-the-first-black-player-in-organized-baseball/

## 1896 — John Matthew Shippen Jr.
- **Exact first:** John Matthew Shippen Jr. was the first Black professional golfer in the United States and the first U.S.-born golf professional, competing as a professional in the 1896 U.S. Open at Shinnecock Hills, where he tied for fifth.
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** STAGED in this packet — PROPOSED CTL-E-7407 (Person). Not in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** Stands in substance. The qualifiers (Black / U.S.-born) are part of the claim; "first professional golfer" unqualified would not survive, as British-born professionals were already working in the U.S.
- **Source:** PGA Tour, "Remembering John Shippen" — Shippen's $10 prize "officially secured his place in history as the first U.S.-born golf professional and the first Black golf professional"; the USGA recognizes him as America's first golf professional. https://www.pgatour.com/article/news/latest/2021/06/26/remembering-john-shippen-the-namesake-for-the-john-shippen-invitational-was-one-of-golfs-groundbreakers
- **Source:** Metropolitan Golf Association, "John Matthew Shippen, Jr." (MGA Honors) — first African American golf professional and first American-born pro to compete in the U.S. Open. https://mgahonors.org/honorees/john-matthew-shippen-jr-2020/

## 1899 — Marshall W. "Major" Taylor
- **Exact first:** Marshall Walter "Major" Taylor was the first African American world champion in cycling, winning the one-mile sprint at the 1899 world track championships in Montreal.
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** HELD — CTL-E-6903 (Person). Also in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** Stands as written. Framing guard: Taylor was the second Black athlete to win a world championship in any sport (after Canadian boxer George Dixon, 1890/91) — never render as "first Black world champion" without the cycling / African American form.
- **Source:** Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Major Taylor" — Taylor "was the 1899 world champion in cycling," winning in Montreal. https://www.britannica.com/biography/Major-Taylor

## 1904 — George Poage
- **Exact first:** George Coleman Poage was the first African American to win an Olympic medal, winning bronze medals in the 200-metre and 400-metre hurdles at the 1904 St. Louis Summer Olympics.
- **Grade:** Verified — in the African American form. The unqualified global form ("first Black athlete to win an Olympic medal") does not survive: Constantin Henriquez won rugby gold with France at Paris 1900 (see 04-red-team.md).
- **Graph status:** STAGED in this packet — PROPOSED CTL-E-7408 (Person). Not in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** Stands with the American qualifier made explicit; the qualifier is part of the claim.
- **Source:** U.S. Olympic & Paralympic Museum, "George Poage was the first Black American to win an Olympic medal" — Poage "placed third in both the 200-meter hurdles and 400-meter hurdles… becoming the first Black American Olympic medalist." https://usopm.org/with-little-fanfare-george-poage-was-the-first-black-american-to-win-an-olympic-medal/
- **Source:** Olympedia, "400 metres Hurdles, Men" (1904) — Poage's bronze was "the first individual Olympic medal won by a Black athlete." https://www.olympedia.org/results/56138

## 1908 — John Baxter Taylor Jr.
- **Exact first:** John Baxter Taylor Jr. was the first African American to win an Olympic gold medal, running the 400-metre leg of the United States' winning 1,600-metre medley relay team at the 1908 London Summer Olympics.
- **Grade:** Verified — in the African American form. The unqualified global form does not survive (Henriquez, 1900 — see 04-red-team.md).
- **Graph status:** STAGED in this packet — PROPOSED CTL-E-7409 (Person). Not in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** Stands. Taylor's gold was a relay (team) gold; the first African American individual gold is Hubbard, 1924. The event was the medley relay — Team USA's article mislabels it the "4x400-meter relay"; do not propagate that label. The intake's "Doc" nickname reflects his 1908 veterinary doctorate but is not his standard name in authoritative sources.
- **Source:** World Athletics, "Fact Friday: John Baxter Taylor" — first African American to win an Olympic gold medal; born November 3, 1882, Washington, D.C.; 400-metre leg of the 1,600-metre medley relay, London 1908. https://worldathletics.org/news/news/67-days-countdown-to-world-athletics-u20-championships-2021
- **Source:** Team USA (U.S. Olympic & Paralympic Committee), "A Look At Some Of The History-Making Black Athletes Of Team USA," Feb 15, 2022 — Taylor "came before them all as the first Black athlete to win an Olympic gold medal" at the 1908 London Games. (Cited for the claim, not the event name — see note above.) https://www.teamusa.com/news/2022/february/15/a-look-at-some-of-the-history-making-black-athletes-of-team-usa

## 1908 — Jack Johnson
- **Exact first:** Jack Johnson was the first African American world heavyweight boxing champion, winning the title from Tommy Burns in Sydney, Australia, on December 26, 1908.
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** HELD — CTL-E-5508 (Person). Also in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** Stands as written. The claim is safe only in its heavyweight form — Joe Gans (lightweight, 1902) and George Dixon (1890s) were earlier Black champions at other weights.
- **Source:** Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Jack Johnson" — Johnson "was the first African American to become heavyweight champion… He won the title by knocking out champion Tommy Burns in Sydney on December 26, 1908." https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jack-Johnson

## 1921 — Fritz Pollard
- **Exact first:** Fritz Pollard became the first African American head coach in NFL (then American Professional Football Association) history when he was named co-head coach of the Akron Pros in 1921.
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** HELD — CTL-E-5871 (Person). Also in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** CORRECTED. Intake read "1922–1937." The coaching first is 1921 (the league was not renamed NFL until 1922, the likely source of the error). Pollard's NFL playing/coaching ran to 1926; the later years in the intake span were coaching all-Black barnstorming teams (Chicago Black Hawks; New York Brown Bombers), not NFL coaching. "Co-head coach" (with Elgie Tobin) is part of the exact claim.
- **Source:** Pro Football Hall of Fame, "Pioneer" — "in 1921, Pollard… was named co-coach of the Pros and hence became the first African American head coach in league history." https://www.profootballhof.com/news/pioneer
- **Source:** Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Fritz Pollard" — "the first African American head coach in the National Football League (NFL), with the Akron Pros in 1921." https://www.britannica.com/biography/Fritz-Pollard

## 1921 — Shady Rest Golf and Country Club
- **Exact first:** The Shady Rest Golf and Country Club in Scotch Plains, New Jersey, established in 1921, was the first African American country club in the United States.
- **Year:** 1921
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** STAGED in this packet — PROPOSED CTL-E-7410 (Organization). Not in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** CORRECTED. Intake read "Oldest African American golf club in the US." Sources support "first," not "oldest" — a different and harder claim — and support "country club," not the narrower "golf club."
- **Source:** Township of Scotch Plains, NJ, "Shady Rest Golf and Country Club Joins National Register of Historic Places," Aug 3, 2022 — the National Park Service granted "the Shady Rest Golf and Country Club – the first African-American country club in the United States – a listing on the National Register of Historic Places." https://www.scotchplainsnj.gov/index.php/news/766-shady-rest-golf-and-country-club-joins-national-register-of-historic-places
- **Source:** New Jersey Council for the Humanities, "Preserve Shady Rest" — "Shady Rest Golf and Country Club opened in 1921 and operated as the first Black-owned country club in the United States until 1938." https://njhumanities.org/grantee/preserve-shady-rest/

## 1923 — Rajo Jack (Dewey Gatson)
- **Exact first:** Dewey Gatson, who raced as Jack DeSoto and became famous as Rajo Jack, was one of the first African American professional race-car drivers in the United States, racing on his own from about 1923 after beginning at fairgrounds in the early 1920s.
- **Grade:** Provisional — no authoritative source states a sole-first claim; the strongest sources say "one of the first," and the start date itself varies (1920 / 1923 / by 1925 across sources).
- **Graph status:** STAGED in this packet — PROPOSED CTL-E-7411 (Person). Not in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** CORRECTED in form and certainty. Intake asserted a singular first ("Professional Race Car Driver: Rajo Jack De Soto, 1923"). Sources support "one of the first," not "the first," and 1923 is a start window, not a proven first date. Name forms vary: Dewey Gatson / Jack DeSoto / Rajo Jack.
- **Source:** African American Automotive Association (AAAA) Hall of Fame — Rajo Jack was "one of America's first African American racers"; began racing at fairgrounds in 1920; "From 1923–1954 he would dominate the 'Outlaw Circuits'." https://www.aaaassoc.com/hall-of-fame
- **Source:** INDYCAR, "African American Pioneer 'Rajo Jack' Continues To Inspire," Feb 8, 2022 — pioneer status, 30+ outlaw wins, barred from the Indianapolis 500; does not date a 1923 debut or call him the first. https://www.indycar.com/news/2022/02/02-08-rajo-jack
- **Source:** Hemmings, "Racing for Recognition" (2018), citing historian Larry Ball Jr. — "a lot of Rajo Jack's life history has been clouded by the passage of years"; born Dewey Gatson, July 28, 1905, Tyler, Texas; racing by 1925 in the Pacific Northwest. https://www.hemmings.com/stories/racing-for-recognition/

## 1924 — William DeHart Hubbard
- **Exact first:** William DeHart Hubbard was the first African American to win an Olympic gold medal in an individual event, winning the long jump at the 1924 Paris Summer Olympics.
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** STAGED in this packet — PROPOSED CTL-E-7412 (Person). Not in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.

## 1947 — Jackie Robinson
- **Exact first:** Jackie Robinson was the first African American to play in a major-league (American League / National League) game in the 20th century, debuting for the Brooklyn Dodgers on April 15, 1947 and breaking modern baseball's color barrier.
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** HELD — CTL-E-5415 (Person). Also in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.

- **Source:** National Baseball Hall of Fame, "#Shortstops: A Page in Time" — Robinson was "the first African-American player to play in the big leagues in the 20th century." https://baseballhall.org/discover/shortstops/a-page-in-time

## 1953 — Willie Thrower
- **Exact first:** Willie Thrower was the first Black quarterback to play quarterback in a modern-era NFL game, appearing for the Chicago Bears against the San Francisco 49ers on October 18, 1953.
- **Grade:** Supported — the Bears state an unqualified form, but earlier position-definition evidence (Pollard 1923, Taliaferro, Washington) prevents a Verified grade; the claim ships only with "modern-era."
- **Graph status:** HELD — CTL-E-5936 (Person). Also in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** CORRECTED by qualifier. Intake's bare "NFL quarterback, 1953" must carry "modern NFL / modern era." Detail discipline: Thrower threw passes in one game (Oct 18, 1953; 3 of 8 for 27 yards relieving George Blanda); sources differ on whether he appeared in one further game without throwing.
- **Source:** Chicago Bears, "African-American pioneers in Bears history" — "Thrower became the first African-American quarterback in NFL history on Oct. 18, 1953 when he replaced an ineffective George Blanda in a loss to the 49ers at Wrigley Field." (Team source; unqualified form noted, not shipped.) https://www.chicagobears.com/news/african-american-pioneers-in-bears-history
- **Source:** Chicago Tribune (syndicated), "Chicago Bears: Willie Thrower was modern NFL's 1st Black quarterback" — "the first Black quarterback to play at a Big Ten school and in the modern NFL," debut Oct 18, 1953. https://www.edinburgpost.com/chicago-bears-willie-thrower-was-modern-nfls-1st-black-quarterback/

## 1957 — Althea Gibson
- **Exact first:** Althea Gibson was the first Black player to win the Wimbledon ladies' singles championship, in 1957.
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** HELD — CTL-E-6887 (Person). Also in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** Stands only in the singles form. Precedence fact: Gibson won the French Championships singles in 1956 — the first Black player to win a Grand Slam title — and was Wimbledon doubles champion in 1956, so an unqualified "first Black Wimbledon champion, 1957" is a year late and attackable on CTL's own sources. "Singles" is part of the claim.
- **Source:** International Tennis Hall of Fame, Althea Gibson inductee page — in 1957 Gibson "defeated Darlene Hard in 50 minutes to become the first African-American to win a Wimbledon Ladies Singles Championship"; her major singles titles begin with "the French in 1956." https://www.tennisfame.com/hall-of-famers/inductees/althea-gibson

- **Source:** NHL.com, "Jan. 18: O'Ree first black player in NHL" — "Willie O'Ree becomes the first black player to appear in an NHL game when he debuts for the Boston Bruins' game against the Montreal Canadiens at the Forum." https://www.nhl.com/news/this-date-in-nhl-history-january-18-285722216

## 1963 — Wendell Oliver Scott
- **Exact first:** Wendell Oliver Scott was the first Black driver to win a NASCAR premier-series (Grand National) race, winning at Speedway Park, Jacksonville, Florida, on December 1, 1963.
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** HELD — CTL-E-6923 (Person). Also in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** Stands in the first-to-WIN form; "major race" tightened to "premier-series (Grand National)." Record note: Scott was initially scored third and Buck Baker went through victory-lane ceremonies; a recheck found Scott had won by two laps, and his family received a trophy only in 2021. Never convert to "first to compete" — Elias Bowie (1955) and Charlie Scott (1956) started premier-series races before him.
- **Source:** NASCAR Hall of Fame, "Wendell Scott's 1962 Chevrolet" — Scott was "the first Black driver to win a NASCAR race. On December 1, 1963, Scott drove a year-old Chevrolet to victory in the Jacksonville (Florida) 200 at Speedway Park." https://www.nascarhall.com/blog/wendell-scotts-1962-chevrolet
- **Source:** Smithsonian National Museum of American History, "The Black American Racers fought for equity in auto racing" — Scott won at Jacksonville in 1963, "but earlier racers Elias Bowie and Charlie Scott entered NASCAR races in 1955 and 1956, respectively." https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/equity-auto-racing

## 1966 — Texas Western Miners
- **Exact first:** The Texas Western College Miners were the first team to win the NCAA men's basketball championship with an all-Black starting five, defeating Kentucky 72–65 on March 19, 1966.
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** STAGED in this packet — PROPOSED CTL-E-7413 (Organization). Not in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** CORRECTED outright. Intake read "First predominantly black basketball team to win an NCAA championship." No source frames it that way, and the roster itself refutes it (seven Black players, four white players, one Hispanic player). The sourced first is the all-Black starting five. Loyola Chicago (1963) had already won the title starting four Black players — "all-Black starting five" is the load-bearing form.
- **Source:** NCAA.com, Andy Katz, "How Texas Western's 1966 national championship changed college sports forever," March 19, 2020 — "Texas Western and head coach Don Haskins started five African-American players in the nationally televised championship game… the first time a team started five African-American players in the title game." https://www.ncaa.com/news/basketball-men/article/2020-03-19/how-texas-westerns-1966-national-championship-changed?amp
- **Source:** University of Texas at El Paso, "UTEP Celebrates 50th Anniversary of Historic 1966 NCAA Championship Win" — "Coach Don Haskins made history by starting five African-American players, the first time that had been done in an NCAA basketball championship game." http://www.utep.edu/glory-road/the-anniversary/

## 1986 — George Branham III
- **Exact first:** George Branham III was the first African American to win a PBA Tour title, winning the Brunswick Memorial World Open on November 22, 1986, in Glendale Heights, Illinois, defeating Mark Roth 195–191.
- **Year:** 1986 — the intake year, 1985, is REFUTED (see intake note).
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** HELD — CTL-E-6929 (Person, whose record already carries the PBA and USBC sources below). Also in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** CORRECTED — the package's clearest factual correction. No retrieved source supports a Branham PBA title in 1985; 1985 was the year he first competed in a PBA Tour event (FloBowling). Two traps in the secondary literature are fenced off: Encyclopedia.com's entry wrongly opens by dating his first title to 1993 (that was his first *major*, the Firestone Tournament of Champions), and BlackPast loosely calls the 1986 World Open a "major" — the PBA itself separates the 1986 first title from the 1993 first major. Both firsts are real; they are different claims and are never merged.
- **Source:** Professional Bowlers Association, "Rash, O'Neill, Branham, Schroeder make up PBA Hall of Fame Class of 2025" — "Branham was the first African American to win a PBA Tour title… Branham won his first title in the 1986 Brunswick Memorial World Open." https://www.pba.com/2025/february/rash-oneill-branham-schroeder-make-pba-hall-fame-class-2025
- **Source:** United States Bowling Congress (bowl.com), "As the Crowe Flies," Dec 18, 2024 — "Branham became the first black PBA champion on the national tour when he defeated PBA great Mark Roth to win the 1986 Brunswick Memorial World Open in Glendale Heights, Illinois." https://bowl.com/news/as-the-crowe-flies
- **Source:** FloBowling, "Bowling's First Black PBA Champion George Branham III Was 'A Natural,'" Feb 15, 2021 — "He competed in his first PBA Tour event in 1985… in year two, he made history… defeating his bowling hero, Mark Roth, 195-191, at the 1986 Brunswick Memorial World Open." https://www.flobowling.com/articles/6888458-bowlings-first-black-pba-champion-george-branham-iii-was-a-natural

## 1997 — Tiger Woods
- **Exact first:** Tiger Woods was the first Black golfer to win a major championship, winning the 1997 Masters Tournament.
- **Grade:** Verified
- **Graph status:** HELD — CTL-E-6890 (Person). Also in the Afro American Encyclopedia of Sports roster v2.
- **Intake note:** CORRECTED by qualifier. Intake's "Golf champion" is imprecise; "major championship" is load-bearing — Charlie Sifford, Pete Brown and Lee Elder won regular PGA Tour events decades earlier, and Elder played the Masters from 1975. Framing note: contemporaneous reporting and Woods's own 1997 words ("I'm the first, but I wasn't the pioneer. Charlie Sifford, Lee Elder, Teddy Rhodes, those guys paved the way for me") use the first-Black/African-American form; the register cites that record. Woods's multiracial heritage is documented in the same sources and is not suppressed.
- **Source:** Golf Digest, "The Dawn of Dominance: An oral history of Tiger Woods' 1997 Masters win" — "Tiger became the first Black golfer to win a major and the youngest player, at 21, to win at Augusta National." https://www.golfdigest.com/story/the-dawn-of-dominance-oral-history-of-tiger-woods-97-win
- **Source:** NBC Sports, contemporary report, Apr 14, 1997 — "Woods' victory had already transcended the fact that he was the first black to win a major professional golf championship." https://www.nbcsports.com/golf/news/tiger-woods-wins-1997-masters-historic-fashion
- **Source:** Encyclopaedia Britannica, Tiger Woods summary — "In 1997 Woods at age 21 became the youngest player and the first of African or Asian descent ever to win the Masters Tournament." https://www.britannica.com/summary/Tiger-Woods
